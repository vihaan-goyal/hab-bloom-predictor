# Snowball: low-impact biological approaches (plants, wetlands, grazers, filter feeders, biomanipulation)

**Summary.**
1. **Best teen experiment: a Daphnia grazing test in jars or tanks.** Use *Daphnia magna* from a biology supplier and a non-toxic green alga or non-toxic cyanobacterium, at several Daphnia densities, and measure chlorophyll or turbidity over 1-3 weeks. Field enclosures show Daphnia cutting phytoplankton by 74-80% or more, even in toxic blooms (#38, #39).
2. **Second: plant-versus-algae jars.** Use hornwort (*Ceratophyllum*) from an aquarium shop, and plastic plants as the control for shading. Or run a dose-response with watercress (*Nasturtium officinale*) extract (1% extract cut *Microcystis* 75.7% in 8 days; water-crowfoot extract, 100%), and include low doses to test for hormesis (#7-#9, #15, #17). A marine version uses *Ulva* or *Gracilaria* against a non-toxic marine alga; seaweeds cut several Long Island Sound HAB species by 50-95% (#19-#22).
3. **Third: a mini floating treatment wetland (FTW) in tanks.** Use a foam raft with rushes or reeds, a raft-only control and an open control, and measure chlorophyll, nitrate and phosphate over 4 weeks. A peat-based FTW cut chlorophyll a by 80% in 4 weeks (#1).
4. **Environmental impact is the lowest of any method family:** nothing synthetic is added, and native plants or animals do the work. The real risks are invasive species (zebra and quagga mussels, carp), toxins building up in shellfish and grazers (a food-safety issue), DOC leaching and oxygen loss from decaying plants, and failure when blooms are dense.
5. **Duration:** effects last only while the organisms are present and healthy. They build up over weeks to years, not hours, so this family prevents or suppresses blooms rather than giving emergency kills. Biomanipulation typically needs "some maintenance" (#44) and repeat treatment (#43).

Sources were found through the project CSV (the "Biomanipulation / grazers / filter feeders" category plus a title filter; 381 matches), one citation-snowball level through OpenAlex (papers citing #3, #13, #18 and #25), and web searches. "Abstract only" means I read the abstract (OpenAlex, a publisher page or a repository page) but not the paper.

## A. Floating treatment wetlands and emergent plants

### 1. Peat-based FTW cuts phytoplankton 80% (Jones, Willis, Gough & Freeman, 2016) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecoleng.2016.11.002 (Bangor repository abstract: https://research.bangor.ac.uk/portal/en/researchoutputs/an-experimental-use-of-floating-treatment-wetlands-ftws-to-reduce-phytoplankton-growth-in-freshwaters(dd4fb225-655f-45d7-b69f-d1cb00330525).html)
- **What they did:** peat-based floating wetlands planted with *Phragmites australis* on eutrophic freshwater, in small-scale experimental ponds, for 4 weeks.
- **Environment:** nutrient uptake, plus possibly phenolics from the peat. The authors flag **DOC leaching from the FTW** as a concern.
- **Results:** chlorophyll a **80% lower** than controls at 4 weeks, through uptake of nitrate and phosphate.
- **Effective?** Yes, at small scale; slow (weeks).
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes.** Tubs, a foam or coir raft, rushes or reeds, a fertiliser spike, and a chlorophyll or turbidity reading. Add a raft-without-plants control to separate shading from uptake. **Read:** abstract only.

### 2. FTWs in three Florida stormwater ponds: nutrients, chlorophyll and microcystin (Hartshorn et al., 2016) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chemosphere.2015.08.023 (UCF abstract: https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2015/3225)
- **What they did:** field sampling during storm and non-storm events in three wet detention ponds (Ruskin, Gainesville, Orlando) fitted with FTWs.
- **Environment:** no additives; a correlational field study.
- **Results:** total phosphorus correlated positively with chlorophyll a at most sites, and **negatively with microcystin** in all three ponds. The interactions are complex, and there is no clean before/after effect.
- **Effective?** Partly. It shows FTWs in real ponds but no clear bloom reduction.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Partly: sampling a local stormwater pond with an FTW is possible with test kits. **Read:** abstract only.

### 3. Full-scale FTWs, Wickham Park pond, Florida, 2016-2020 (White, 2021) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3390/w13040569
- **What they did:** 900 m² of FTWs (5% of the pond's surface) on a eutrophic pond (TP 0.13 mg/L, TN 0.96 mg/L) for 4 years, with the plants harvested each year.
- **Environment:** low impact. The benefit depends on **harvesting**; otherwise the nutrients return when the plants die.
- **Results:** 73,000 kg of biomass harvested in 2016-2020, removing **1,026 kg N and 262 kg P**. *Juncus effusus* fixed N at 13.5 g/m²/yr. A mixer increased P uptake. No bloom metric was reported.
- **Effective?** Partly. It removes nutrients (prevention); bloom suppression was not measured.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Partly. Harvest-and-weigh is easy, but plant tissue N and P needs a lab. **Read:** abstract only.

### 4. Artificial floating islands did NOT reduce cyanobacteria, Valle de Bravo reservoir, Mexico (Ramírez & Chicalote-Castillo, 2020) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.4081/jlimnol.2020.1959
- **What they did:** islands planted with *Phragmites* or *Schoenoplectus* in a eutrophic drinking-water reservoir, monitored for 24 months.
- **Environment:** the islands **raised nitrate** and did not change phosphate. The *Phragmites* island favoured *Microcystis*.
- **Results:** cyanobacteria were not reduced. Extracellular microcystin fell at both islands in one rainy season.
- **Effective?** **No** in an open reservoir; the effect depends on the species.
- **Teen-reproducible?** No (field scale), but it is a useful counter-example for the literature review. **Read:** abstract only.

### 5. Free-floating plant wetland on a hypertrophic urban lake, Lake Rodó, Uruguay (Rodríguez-Gallego et al., 2004) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1440-1770.2004.00245.x
- **What they did:** three pools converted to wetlands of water hyacinth (*Eichhornia*) and duckweed (*Spirodela*), with lake water recirculated through them, over several years.
- **Environment:** water hyacinth is **invasive** in US waters. Harvest barely touched sediment nutrients (1-2%).
- **Results:** harvests removed **58-88% of water-column N and 39-78% of water-column P** loads. Algal biomass in the pools was generally lower than in the lake.
- **Effective?** Partly: good for the water column, not for the legacy in the sediment.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes, with **duckweed** (not hyacinth): shade and uptake in tubs of algae, as a duckweed-cover gradient. **Read:** abstract only.

### 6. Constructed floating wetland on a sewage pond, Australia (Huth, Walker, Kulkarni & Lucke, 2021) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3390/w13131746
- **What they did:** 4 CFW islands with several plant species, 375 days, on a waste stabilisation pond.
- **Environment:** low impact; harvest removes the nutrients.
- **Results:** harvest removed about **23.2 kg N and 2.97 kg P**. *Baumea articulata* had the highest uptake (shoots: 104 g N/m²).
- **Effective?** Partly (nutrient removal; algae not measured).
- **Teen-reproducible?** Partly: comparing plant species in tubs is doable. **Read:** abstract only.

## B. Submerged plants (macrophytes) and plant extracts

### 7. Milfoil and hornwort inhibit cyanobacteria; pondweed does not (Körner & Nicklisch, 2002) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1529-8817.2002.t01-1-02001.x
- **What they did:** lab dialysis co-cultures of *Myriophyllum spicatum*, *Ceratophyllum demersum* and *Potamogeton pectinatus* with cyanobacteria, a diatom and a green alga, measured by PAM fluorometry.
- **Environment:** a natural allelopathy mechanism (it targets photosystem II).
- **Results:** milfoil and hornwort inhibited PSII and growth, and the effect grew with plant biomass. Oscillatoriales and *Microcystis* were most sensitive; *Aphanizomenon*, the diatom and *Scenedesmus* were less so. Pondweed had no effect.
- **Effective?** Yes in the lab, and specific to the species.
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes.** Hornwort is a common aquarium plant. Split jars with dialysis tubing or a mesh bag separate allelopathy from shading. **Read:** abstract only.

### 8. *Egeria* and *Potamogeton* suppress phytoplankton; artificial-plant controls (Vanderstukken et al., 2011) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2427.2011.02624.x
- **What they did:** two mesocosm experiments in Uruguay comparing no plants, **artificial plants**, real *Egeria densa* and real *Potamogeton illinoensis*, with zooplankton excluded.
- **Environment:** nothing added.
- **Results:** no physical (shading) effect from the artificial plants. Both real plants had strong biological effects. Only *Egeria* competed for nutrients, and *Egeria* tempered *Scenedesmus* dominance.
- **Effective?** Yes (mesocosm).
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes: the ideal design to copy.** *Egeria* (Brazilian waterweed, sold as "Anacharis") versus plastic aquarium plants versus nothing. Do not release *Egeria*; it is invasive. **Read:** abstract only (the abstract was truncated before the Experiment 2 result).

### 9. Meta-analysis: how submerged plants inhibit algae (Liu et al., 2024) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvman.2024.120480
- **What they did:** meta-analysis of lab and mesocosm studies comparing shading, nutrient competition and allelopathy.
- **Environment:** n/a.
- **Results:** **allelopathy caused stronger inhibition than shading or nutrient competition.** Cyanobacteria and diatoms were inhibited more than green algae, and toxic cyanobacteria more than non-toxic ones. *Ceratophyllum*, *Vallisneria* and *Potamogeton* strongly inhibited *Microcystis*. Co-culture beat filtrate or extract methods.
- **Effective?** Yes, as a pooled lab result; more mesocosm evidence is needed.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Use it to pick the plant (hornwort) and the method (co-culture). **Read:** abstract only.

### 10. Hornwort and milfoil versus toxic and non-toxic *Microcystis* (Tang et al., 2025) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms13122797
- **What they did:** indoor co-cultures of *C. demersum* and *M. spicatum* with toxic and non-toxic *M. aeruginosa*.
- **Environment:** the plants **took up microcystin into their tissue** and suffered oxidative stress from toxic strains.
- **Results:** both plants significantly lowered chlorophyll a, **mainly through rapid N and P uptake** (similar for toxic and non-toxic strains). *C. demersum* removed nutrients and microcystin better.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab).
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes**, with the non-toxic strain only. **Read:** abstract only.

### 11. Hornwort cuts a cyanobacterial bloom 85% in tropical mesocosms (Amorim, Jeppesen & Moura, 2024) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10750-024-05646-8
- **What they did:** a factorial mesocosm in a hypereutrophic Brazilian reservoir during a bloom that was more than 98% cyanobacteria: *Ceratophyllum demersum* × a large cladoceran × nutrients.
- **Environment:** the plants also shifted native zooplankton communities.
- **Results:** macrophytes **reduced cyanobacteria by 85%**, diatoms by 80% and green algae by 78%, even with added nutrients and without extra grazers, at "low to moderate" plant volume.
- **Effective?** Yes (mesocosm, during a real bloom).
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes**: tank × plant × Daphnia is a 2×2 factorial. **Read:** abstract only.

### 12. Plant cover and diversity matter more than biomass in restored lakes (Gao et al., 2020) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3390/w12051425
- **What they did:** transplanted submerged macrophytes into two restored Chinese lakes (5 ha and 8 ha) and tracked them for 1-2 years.
- **Environment:** dense canopy-forming milfoil was **unfavourable** for nutrient control.
- **Results:** coverage and meadow-forming species (*Vallisneria*) improved clarity and lowered nutrients. The lake with higher diversity had better water quality.
- **Effective?** Yes (field, 1-2 years).
- **Teen-reproducible?** Partly: a tank test of mixed versus single-species plantings. **Read:** abstract only.

### 13. Snails plus *Vallisneria* beat either alone (Mo et al., 2017) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1051/kmae/2017034
- **What they did:** a mesocosm with *Vallisneria natans*, the snail *Bellamya aeruginosa*, both, or neither, sampled every 2 weeks.
- **Environment:** native organisms; benthic algae also fell.
- **Results:** plants alone reduced TN and algae; snails alone reduced TN and algae. **The combination** reduced TN, TP and planktonic algae and raised light at the sediment.
- **Effective?** Yes (mesocosm).
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes**: aquarium snails plus *Vallisneria* (both sold for aquaria) in tanks. **Read:** abstract only.

### 14. Pond snails plus pondweed in 10-day simulations (Zhang, Xie, Jiang & Wang, 2014) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1002/clen.201300922
- **What they did:** simulated bloom tanks with the snail *Radix swinhoei*, the plant *Potamogeton lucens*, or both, for 10 days.
- **Environment:** **snails alone increased nutrients** and did not reduce cyanobacteria.
- **Results:** plants alone cut chlorophyll a by 31.5%. **Plants plus snails cut chlorophyll a by 76.2%**, TN by 51.4% and TP by 55.6%.
- **Effective?** Yes, for the combination.
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes**: 10 days, aquarium snails and pondweed. **Read:** abstract only.

### 15. Watercress and water-crowfoot extracts stop *Microcystis* (Tazart, Douma, Tebaa & Loudiki, 2018) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.2166/ws.2018.072
- **What they did:** aqueous extracts of *Ranunculus aquatilis* and ***Nasturtium officinale* (watercress)** at 0-1%, tested on *M. aeruginosa* for 8 days.
- **Environment:** polyphenols, flavonoids and tannins; cells sank at 1%. Non-target effects were not tested.
- **Results:** dose-dependent. At 1% after 8 days, inhibition was **100% (Ranunculus)** and **75.7% (watercress)**.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab).
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes: grocery-store watercress**, a blender, a coffee filter, and a non-toxic cyanobacterium or green alga. **Read:** abstract only.

### 16. Water-crowfoot extract: 82% fewer cells, but some other strains got more toxic (Tazart et al., 2021) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms9081782
- **What they did:** batch tests of *R. aquatilis* extract on *Microcystis* and on bacterial enzyme activity, then on other toxic strains.
- **Environment:** no harm to the associated bacteria. **With *Planktothrix*, *Raphidiopsis* and *Chrysosporum*, growth slowed but toxin content did not, so toxicity increased.**
- **Results:** *M. aeruginosa* density fell by **82.18%**, and microcystin fell and stayed inside the cells.
- **Effective?** Partly: it depends on the species.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes (extract jars). This paper is the reason to measure more than cell counts. **Read:** abstract only.

### 17. Plant extracts: low doses stimulate *Microcystis* (hormesis), high doses inhibit it (Li, Zhao, Chen & Wang, 2023) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecoenv.2023.115703
- **What they did:** extracts of *Ceratophyllum*, *Myriophyllum spicatum* and *Vallisneria* at low and high doses, 48-120 h.
- **Environment:** a low dose can **help** the bloom.
- **Results:** hormesis (stimulation) at low doses and inhibition at high doses, from all three plants.
- **Effective?** Only above a threshold dose.
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes**: a wide dose-response curve is the key design lesson. **Read:** abstract only.

### 18. *Vallisneria* recruits algicidal bacteria to its leaves (Gao et al., 2026) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2025.1731742
- **What they did:** tracked the microbiome on *Vallisneria natans* leaves before, during and after *Microcystis* exposure.
- **Environment:** natural; the bacteria returned to baseline after the stress ended.
- **Results:** possible algicidal bacteria (*Streptomyces*, *Pseudomonas*, *Chryseobacterium*) became enriched on the leaves during the stress phase.
- **Effective?** Explains a mechanism; no control numbers.
- **Teen-reproducible?** No (needs sequencing). **Read:** abstract only.

## C. Marine macrophytes (seaweeds): the most Long Island Sound-relevant plant work

### 19. Sugar kelp, Irish moss and *Ulva* kill *Alexandrium* and cut saxitoxin in mussels (Sylvers & Gobler, 2021) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hal.2021.102056
- **What they did:** co-cultures, bottle incubations of field bloom water, and mesocosms, with *Saccharina latissima*, *Chondrus crispus* and *Ulva* at aquaculture-realistic densities, against *A. catenella* (a Northeast US PSP species).
- **Environment:** living seaweed, native, harvestable. It lessened toxin uptake by blue mussels.
- **Results:** 17-74% fewer cells in 2-3 days and 42-96% in about a week. Mesocosms: kelp cut 73% in 48 h and *Ulva* 54% in 96 h. Mussel STX was **71.8 vs 93.5 µg/100 g** (closure limit 80).
- **Effective?** Yes, at mesocosm scale in cold northeastern waters.
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes**, using *Ulva* (sea lettuce, collectable or from marine aquarium suppliers) with a **non-toxic** marine alga instead of *Alexandrium*. **Read:** abstract only.

### 20. Native *Gracilaria* suppresses brown tide *Aureococcus* (Benitt, Young, Sylvers & Gobler, 2022) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10811-021-02677-9
- **What they did:** co-cultures of *A. anophagefferens* strains with *Gracilaria tikvahiae* (native) or *Dasysiphonia japonica* (invasive) at 0.25-3.00 g/L.
- **Environment:** allelopathy was the main mechanism; bacteria, nutrients and pH mattered less.
- **Results:** dose-dependent cell reductions in nearly all experiments. *Dasysiphonia* was stronger than *Gracilaria* and *Ulva*.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab).
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes** with *Gracilaria* (the native red seaweed of LIS; Yarish lab work at UConn). Brown tide cultures are non-toxic to humans but need a supplier. **Read:** abstract only.

### 21. Seaweeds protect fish from *Margalefidinium* (rust tide) (Sylvers & Gobler, 2023) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.aquaculture.2023.739676
- **What they did:** survival assays of larval forage fish (*Menidia*, *Cyprinodon*) in cultured and bloom *M. polykrikoides*, with *Ulva*, *Gracilaria*, *Chondrus* or *Grateloupia*.
- **Environment:** an antioxidant (DMSP) partly explains the protection.
- **Results:** seaweeds extended survival in all cases, sometimes to control levels. **0.2 g/L *Ulva* cut wild bloom populations by 68-92%** over longer times.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab and bloom water).
- **Teen-reproducible?** Partly: the algae half is yes, but fish work needs IACUC/SRC approval under ISEF vertebrate rules. **Read:** abstract only.

### 22. Kelp and *Ulva* inhibit toxic *Pseudo-nitzschia* (Sylvers & Gobler, 2025) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1002/lno.70127
- **What they did:** co-cultures and field bottle incubations with *S. latissima*, *Ulva* and *Gracilaria*; toxin tests in mussels and razor clams.
- **Environment:** kelp lessened domoic acid uptake by the shellfish.
- **Results:** 13-47% reduction in 24-48 h, and **74-94% by 72 h or later** (not with *Gracilaria*). Field blooms: 69-81% reduction with kelp at 2 g/L.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab and field bottles).
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes, with a non-toxic diatom substitute. **Read:** abstract only.

### 23. Seagrass and *Ulva* versus benthic and planktonic HAB dinoflagellates (Ben Gharbia et al., 2017) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0187963
- **What they did:** lab co-cultures of fresh *Zostera noltei* and *Cymodocea nodosa* leaves and *Ulva rigida* with *Ostreopsis*, *Prorocentrum lima*, *Coolia* and *Alexandrium pacificum*.
- **Environment:** natural allelopathy.
- **Results:** ***Ulva* was the strongest**, and planktonic *A. pacificum* the most vulnerable. Benthic species were more tolerant.
- **Effective?** Partly (depends on the species pair).
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes (leaf weight gradient in jars). **Read:** abstract only.

### 24. Sugar kelp farming removes nitrogen in Long Island Sound (Kim, Kraemer & Yarish, 2015) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3354/meps11331
- **What they did:** grew *S. latissima* at the Bronx River Estuary, Fairfield CT and Branford CT, winter-spring 2012-13.
- **Environment:** nothing added; the kelp is harvested. Could qualify for CT Nitrogen Credit Trading.
- **Results:** up to **180 / 67 / 38 kg N per ha** (Bronx / western LIS / central LIS), with modelled values of up to $1,600 / $760 / $430 per ha.
- **Effective?** Yes for nutrient extraction (prevention); blooms were not measured.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Partly: a seaweed nitrate-uptake tank test with aquarium test kits. **Read:** abstract only.

### 25. Sugar kelp with oysters in Rhode Island salt ponds (Green, Thornber & Oczkowski, 2023) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3389/faquc.2023.1147524
- **What they did:** kelp longlines at 4 oyster-farm sites, 2017-2019.
- **Environment:** low impact; the permitting barrier is lower than for shellfish.
- **Results:** blade yield 0.36-11.26 kg per m of line; N extraction **0.28-16.35 g N per m of line**; large variation between years and sites.
- **Effective?** Partly: crop consistency is a challenge.
- **Teen-reproducible?** No (farm scale). **Read:** abstract only.

## D. Shellfish filtration in Long Island Sound and nearby estuaries

### 26. Hard-clam spawner sanctuaries ended brown tides, Shinnecock Bay NY (Gobler et al., 2022) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3389/fmars.2022.911731
- **What they did:** planted **3.2 million adult hard clams** (~27 per m²) in 64 no-harvest plots, 2012-2019, with monitoring through 2021.
- **Environment:** native species, no additives. Eelgrass gained ~4.7 × 10⁵ m². Nearby unrestored bays got worse.
- **Results:** bay filtration time fell from up to ~3 months to as low as 10 days. Brown tide peaked at 0.3-1.9 × 10⁶ cells/mL in 2007-2016; **no brown tides from 2017 to 2021** (below 10,000 cells/mL). Chlorophyll a fell significantly.
- **Effective?** **Yes: the strongest field evidence in this family**, lasting 5+ years. It is correlational at the whole-bay scale.
- **Teen-reproducible?** No for the field part. A clam clearance-rate jar test is possible (#29). **Read:** full text (through a summarising fetch tool).

### 27. Oyster aquaculture removes 1.3-2.7% of LIS nutrient inputs (Bricker et al., 2018) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.7b03970 (PMC: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/5784850)
- **What they did:** eutrophication, circulation and farm-scale (FARM) models of Connecticut oyster aquaculture, with an avoided-cost valuation.
- **Environment:** a benefit (bioextraction).
- **Results:** **1.31% (current) and 2.68% (expanded)** of incoming nutrients removed, worth $8.5M and $470M per year. An optimistic scenario of all leases plus denitrification gives 10-30%.
- **Effective?** Partly: a real but small share for the whole Sound.
- **Teen-reproducible?** No, but the NOAA oyster nutrient calculator is usable (#48). **Read:** abstract only.

### 28. An oyster nursery (FLUPSY) did not dent phytoplankton; *Cochlodinium* stopped oyster feeding (Li, Meseck, Dixon, Rivara et al., 2012) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.2983/035.031.0419
- **What they did:** 15-min inflow and outflow measurements at a floating upweller in East Creek, Peconic Estuary NY, June-September 2010.
- **Environment:** harmful algae **depressed oyster feeding and growth and increased mortality.**
- **Results:** median clearance 0.95 L/h/g (range 0.32-2.21, with a diel cycle). "Did not have a large impact on the abundance of phytoplankton."
- **Effective?** **No** at nursery scale during a bloom.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Partly (see #29). **Read:** abstract only.

### 29. Hard clams clear *Margalefidinium* only at moderate densities (de Silva & Gobler, 2023) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3389/fmars.2023.1252540
- **What they did:** clearance-rate trials with 10-20 mm juvenile hard clams on 3 *M. polykrikoides* populations and non-harmful algae.
- **Environment:** grazing collapses in dense blooms.
- **Results:** clams cleared the HAB at **≤1,000 cells/mL**. At ≥1,500 cells/mL, clearance was **not different from zero**.
- **Effective?** Partly: prevention at early bloom stages, not a cure.
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes, with a non-toxic alga.** Seed clams from a CT or NY hatchery, beakers of seawater, *Rhodomonas* or *Tisochrysis* cultures, and a before/after chlorophyll or cell count. Invertebrates need no IACUC. **Read:** abstract only.

### 30. Oysters, mussels and clams eat toxic *Dinophysis* and store its toxins, NY estuaries (McGuire, Sanderson, Smith & Gobler, 2024) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hal.2024.102745
- **What they did:** in-situ clearance rates and toxin tracking during 2021-2022 blooms (10²-10⁵ cells/L) at 3 NY sites.
- **Environment:** **shellfish become a food-safety vector** (diarrhetic shellfish toxins, pectenotoxins).
- **Results:** oysters cleared *D. acuminata* at 1.69 L/h/g, versus 0.46 for mussels and 0.41 for clams. Mussels held the most DST.
- **Effective?** They do graze it, but toxin build-up is the non-target cost.
- **Teen-reproducible?** No (toxin analysis). **Read:** abstract only.

## E. Freshwater bivalves and snails

### 31. Quagga mussels in urban pond enclosures: chlorophyll −97% (Waajen et al., 2016) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecoleng.2016.01.036 (KNAW abstract: https://pure.knaw.nl/portal/en/publications/biomanipulation-with-quagga-mussels-dreissena-rostriformis-bugens/)
- **What they did:** 8 × 750 L enclosures in eutrophic Dutch urban ponds, with 0.3 g wet weight of mussels per L, for 14 days.
- **Environment:** **quagga mussels are invasive**; the authors recommend them only where they are already present. Soluble P rose.
- **Results:** total chlorophyll a **2.4 vs 84.1 µg/L**; cyanobacterial chlorophyll 1.0 vs 7.3; TP 0.08 vs 0.17 mg/L. Microcystin: no effect.
- **Effective?** Yes, short term.
- **Teen-reproducible?** No: **do not use Dreissena** (illegal to transport in CT). Use a native unionid only with permits, or use Daphnia. **Read:** abstract only.

### 32. Zebra mussels clear toxic and non-toxic cyanobacteria equally (Dionísio Pires, Bontes, van Donk & Ibelings, 2005) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1093/plankt/fbi008
- **What they did:** lab grazing with colonial *Microcystis* and filamentous *Planktothrix* (toxic and non-toxic strains).
- **Environment:** the cells come out as pseudofaeces, which are deposited, not destroyed.
- **Results:** all strains were cleared; toxic *Planktothrix* was cleared fastest.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab).
- **Teen-reproducible?** No (invasive organism). **Read:** abstract only.

### 33. Zebra mussels are linked to MORE *Microcystis* in low-nutrient lakes (Raikow, Sarnelle, Wilson & Hamilton, 2004) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.4319/lo.2004.49.2.0482
- **What they did:** a survey of 61 Michigan lakes with and without *Dreissena*.
- **Environment:** **the filter feeder can select for the toxic, grazing-resistant cyanobacterium.**
- **Results:** mussel lakes had lower total phytoplankton, but *Microcystis* dominance was higher where TP was below ~25 µg/L.
- **Effective?** **Backfires** in some lakes.
- **Teen-reproducible?** n/a. The key counter-example. **Read:** abstract only.

### 34. Freshwater clams accumulate microcystin; oysters do not purge it (Prepas et al., 1997; Straquadine, Kudela & Gobler, 2022) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1139/f96-261 ; https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hal.2022.102236 (open access)
- **What they did:** 1997: *Anodonta* clams placed in 3 Alberta lakes for 12-28 days. 2022: Asian clams and eastern oysters fed a Lake Erie *Microcystis* strain or water from Lake Agawam (NY).
- **Environment:** a toxin route to muskrats, predators and people. Clams depurated 69-88% in 6 days; **oysters did not depurate over 3-4 days.**
- **Results:** clams held 24-527 ng/g MC-LReq. Oysters and clams held **>3 µg/g in 24-72 h** on culture, but only ≤2 ng/g on colonial bloom water.
- **Effective?** n/a. This is the main non-target risk of shellfish-based control.
- **Teen-reproducible?** No. **Read:** abstract only.

### 35. Native snail *Bellamya* controls low-density *Microcystis* and purges toxin (Wang et al., 2023) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecoenv.2023.114596
- **What they did:** lab exposure to toxic and non-toxic *M. aeruginosa*, then depuration.
- **Environment:** the snails accumulated up to 3,090 ng/g in the hepatopancreas, then excreted over 80% on clean food.
- **Results:** effective below 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL only.
- **Effective?** Partly (low densities, i.e. early season).
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes**, using a common aquarium snail and a non-toxic alga. **Read:** abstract only.

### 36. Bivalve filtration in 5 L containers: clams 94%, oysters 87%, mussels 77% (Meitei et al., 2025) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s43621-025-01104-0
- **What they did:** 9 × 5 L microcosms with *Thalassiosira* at 1 bivalve per 2 L for 5 days; 300 L tanks with shrimp-farm wastewater.
- **Environment:** waste valorisation; bacterial counts changed with species.
- **Results:** plankton filtration efficiency after 5 days was **94% (blood clam), 87.1% (oyster), 77.0% (green mussel)**.
- **Effective?** Yes (microcosm).
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes: a ready-made protocol** to copy with CT hatchery oyster or clam seed. **Read:** abstract only.

### 37. Pond mussel *Hyriopsis cumingii* clears ponds with a density-dependent effect (Yu et al., 2021) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3390/w13030297
- **What they did:** short-term density trials and long-term monitoring in a recirculating aquaculture pond.
- **Environment:** reduced COD, TN and TP as well.
- **Results:** at high density, TSS fell 22%, particulate organic carbon 57% and phytoplankton and chlorophyll a significantly; transparency improved over the long term.
- **Effective?** Yes (pond).
- **Teen-reproducible?** Partly: use a native mussel only with a permit. **Read:** abstract only.

## F. Zooplankton grazers and biomanipulation

### 38. Daphnia suppress toxic blooms despite high microcystin (Chislock, Sarnelle, Jernigan & Wilson, 2013) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.watres.2012.12.038 (PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23395484)
- **What they did:** limnocorral enclosures in hypereutrophic ponds in Michigan and Alabama, 5 weeks, with *D. pulicaria* clones from eutrophic lakes.
- **Environment:** grazers only; no additives.
- **Results:** Michigan (~96% toxic cyanobacteria, microcystin ~3 µg/L): phytoplankton **−74%** by week 3, sustained. Alabama (microcystin >100 µg/L): **>80% reduction**.
- **Effective?** Yes, if the Daphnia genotypes are adapted to cyanobacteria.
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes**: Daphnia in 5-20 L buckets with green water. **Read:** abstract (through a search-engine summary; not the full text).

### 39. Large Daphnia versus a natural cyanobacterial community (Urrutia-Cordero, Ekvall & Hansson, 2016) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0153032
- **What they did:** three summer grazing experiments with a gradient of *D. magna* biomass on lake water, plus 19 years of monitoring in a lake biomanipulated since 2005.
- **Environment:** one filamentous species (*Dolichospermum crassum*) **grew better** under grazing when it dominated.
- **Results:** total cyanobacteria fell with Daphnia biomass (*Aphanizomenon*, *Microcystis*, *Planktothrix*). After fish reduction, Daphnia were 50% more abundant and 20% larger.
- **Effective?** Mostly yes; the response depends on the taxon.
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes: the best-matched design** (a Daphnia density gradient in jars of pond water). **Read:** abstract only.

### 40. Daphnia graze toxic *Planktothrix* but pass the toxin up the food chain (Oberhaus et al., 2007) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1093/plankt/fbm062
- **What they did:** clearance rates of adult and juvenile *D. pulicaria* on two toxic *Planktothrix* strains, measured in the lab.
- **Environment:** "large quantities" of microcystin found in fed Daphnia, a route to fish.
- **Results:** both strains grazed, more slowly than on *Scenedesmus*. Only short filaments (<100 µm) were preferred, so bloom control is limited.
- **Effective?** Partly.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes, with non-toxic filaments. **Read:** abstract only.

### 41. Pond ultrasound devices kill Daphnia and turn water greener (Lürling & Tolman, 2014) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3390/w6113247
- **What they did:** commercial transducers at 20-44 kHz on *D. magna* in 0.8-3.2 L; 25-day trial in 85 L tanks.
- **Environment:** a **non-target kill of grazers**, relevant to Part 1 #16.
- **Results:** all Daphnia died within 10-135 min. In tanks, the controls cleared as Daphnia flourished; **the ultrasound tanks stayed green.**
- **Effective?** No (for ultrasound). It shows grazers are the natural control.
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes**: Daphnia plus green water in tanks, 25 days. **Read:** abstract only.

### 42. Four days of fish predation wrecks zooplankton control for weeks (Ersoy et al., 2019) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0212351
- **What they did:** 10 mesocosms, juvenile perch at ~20 g/m³ for 4 days, then fish removed; followed for 4 weeks.
- **Environment:** shows how fragile grazer control is.
- **Results:** large zooplankton never returned; small grazers took over and **phytoplankton escaped control** despite high total zooplankton biomass.
- **Effective?** n/a (shows a limit).
- **Teen-reproducible?** Partly (no fish; simulate by removing large Daphnia). **Read:** abstract only.

### 43. Lake Væng: fish removed twice over 30 years (Søndergaard, Lauridsen, Johansson & Jeppesen, 2017) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3390/w9010043
- **What they did:** removed roach and bream from a 16 ha Danish lake twice, 20 years apart.
- **Environment:** a big food-web change; the lake shifted to plants (*Elodea* covering 70-80%).
- **Results:** chlorophyll a 60-80 fell to **10-30 µg/L**; TP 0.12 to 0.07 mg/L; TN 1.0 to 0.4 mg/L. A return to turbid water is likely, so repeat removals may be needed.
- **Effective?** Yes, but temporary (about a decade per treatment).
- **Teen-reproducible?** No. **Read:** abstract only.

### 44. Biomanipulation works, "but maintenance is required" (McQueen, 1998) [peer-reviewed review]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1440-1770.1998.tb00035.x
- **What they did:** reviewed planktivore additions, removals and piscivore additions in deep versus shallow lakes.
- **Environment:** a large food-web change.
- **Results:** removals in deep lakes let ungrazable algae return; removals in shallow lakes are more stable but deteriorate over time. Piscivore additions give only short control.
- **Effective?** Partly.
- **Teen-reproducible?** No. **Read:** abstract only.

### 45. "Nontraditional" biomanipulation with silver and bighead carp (Chen et al., 2023) [peer-reviewed review, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.59717/j.xinn-life.2023.100038
- **What they did:** reviewed 42 experiments with filter-feeding carp, including a whole-lake stocking in Lake Donghu.
- **Environment:** **these carp are invasive in the US** (Mississippi basin), and they can increase small algae.
- **Results:** cyanobacteria fell in **88% of cases**. Donghu *Microcystis* cover went from **87% (2021) to 0% (2022)**.
- **Effective?** Yes in China; unacceptable in US waters.
- **Teen-reproducible?** No. **Read:** abstract only.

### 46. Dutch survey of in-lake bloom measures (Lürling & Mucci, 2020) [peer-reviewed review, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10750-020-04297-9
- **What they did:** reviewed peer-reviewed and grey literature on Dutch in-lake interventions.
- **Environment:** notes that dreissenid mussels run into **legal restrictions**.
- **Results:** "fish introduction" and "effective micro-organisms" had no or limited effect. Continuing diffuse nutrient loads mean interventions have to be repeated.
- **Effective?** A review.
- **Teen-reproducible?** n/a. **Read:** abstract only.

## G. Agency sources and a non-target check on clay

### 47. EPA control-measures page lists no biological controls except barley straw (EPA, updated 2026-01-14) [agency]
- **Link:** https://www.epa.gov/habs/control-measures-cyanobacterial-habs-surface-water
- **What it says:** barley straw is the only biological measure listed. It "does not kill existing algae", takes 2-8 weeks to act, and can cause fish kills by using up oxygen. Floating wetlands appear on the separate *preventative* page (Part 1 #20). Biomanipulation, macrophytes and bivalves are not covered.
- **Effective?** n/a. **Teen-reproducible?** n/a. **Read:** full page.

### 48. NOAA validation of oyster nitrogen removal at Greenwich and Milford, CT (NOAA NCCOS/NEFSC, 2019) [agency]
- **Link:** https://coastalscience.noaa.gov/news/nccos-and-nefsc-scientists-join-industry-and-community-partners-for-lab-and-field-experiments-to-confirm-bioextractive-removal-of-nitrogen-by-oysters/
- **What they did:** field sampling of oysters, water and biodeposits (October 2019) with Stella Mar Oyster Co. and the Greenwich Shellfish Commission, to validate the FARM model.
- **Results:** no numbers on the page. Related NOAA pages say LIS shellfish aquaculture removes **<2% of total N inputs** (up to 3% with expansion).
- **Effective?** Supports #27. **Teen-reproducible?** A possible **mentor contact: the NOAA Milford Lab (Julie Rose)**. **Read:** full page.

### 49. Clay (yellow loess) cuts clearance rates of oysters, mussels and scallops (Shumway, Frank, Ewart & Ward, 2003) [peer-reviewed, open access]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2109.2003.00958.x
- **What they did:** clearance-rate tests of 6 filter-feeding species (including *C. virginica*, *M. edulis* and bay scallop) at loess levels of 0.01-10 g/L.
- **Environment:** **direct evidence for the counselor's objection to clay.**
- **Results:** bay scallops were affected from **0.01 g/L**, mussels at 1-10 g/L, and eastern oysters only at 1.0 g/L. Hydroids were also affected. "Should be approached with extreme caution."
- **Effective?** n/a (a non-target study).
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes, as a clearance-rate method. **Read:** abstract only.
