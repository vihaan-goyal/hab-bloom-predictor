# Snowball: natural compounds (barley and other straws, allelopathy, plant extracts, curcumin, tannins, polyphenols, essential oils)

**Summary (compiled 2026-09-24).**
1. **Best teen experiment #1: a curcumin dose-response in jars with a non-toxic marine alga.** Mote's protocol is simple: food-grade turmeric extract, 0.1-40 mg/L, 1.5 L beakers, cell counts at 0/2/6/24/48 h. Measure colour, dissolved oxygen (DO) and regrowth too, which Mote never fully resolved (#1-#4).
2. **Best teen experiment #2: aged barley straw "tea" versus fresh straw versus wheat straw on a cyanobacterium (or a pond sample) and a green alga.** It tests the lag time and species selectivity, the two biggest unknowns in the field record (#8-#24).
3. **Best teen experiment #3: live seaweed (Ulva / sea lettuce, sugar kelp) in bottles with a marine microalga.** It is the most Long Island Sound-relevant and adds no chemical: the Stony Brook (Gobler lab) work used Long Island *Ulva* and kelp (#28-#30).
4. **Environmental consensus:** plant compounds break down quickly, and the few non-target tests found low harm at working doses: no effect on clams, crabs or urchins over 72 h, no biomarker response in snails, amphipods or fish, no effect on zooplankton. But the effect is **species-specific** (some algae are stimulated), **short-lived** (days for pure compounds, months for straw), and can **raise toxin release** (tannic acid, gallic acid at low dose, juglone per cell). Rotting straw can use up oxygen, and curcumin colours and clouds the water.
5. **Field reality:** barley straw works in many UK reservoirs and canals but failed or was inconsistent in US ponds, a French pond and a brackish Maryland lagoon. Curcumin has only reached 1,400 L tanks. No natural compound has a published open-water marine success.

Entry tags: "full text" = I read the paper's methods and results; "abstract only" = only the abstract; "summary only" = only a database summary (Semantic Scholar TLDR), an agency summary or a news report, so verify before citing numbers.

## A. Curcumin and other red-tide natural products (marine)

### 1. Curcumin against Florida red tide, bench tests (Hall, Heil, Frankle, Klass, Devillier, Lovko, Toyoda & Pierce, 2024) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3390/w16101458 (open access, MDPI *Water* 16:1458)
- **What they did:** 13 lab experiments (2019-2024) with *Karenia brevis* at ~1.0 × 10^6 cells/L; food-grade turmeric extract (>95% curcuminoids, Bulk Supplements) at 0.1-40 mg/L; 1.5 L beakers (48 h) and 10 L aquaria (48-336 h); six stock-preparation methods (deionised water, seawater, heated, 0.001-0.004% ethanol, pure ethanol).
- **Environment:** pH, salinity, temperature and DO stayed in narrow ranges (except DO in the ethanol test); nutrients unchanged in 10 L tanks. Water turns light yellow to dark orange. "White" curcumin (CuroWhite, zedoary) avoided the colour but never exceeded 50% reduction.
- **Results:** 5 mg/L: 89% cell reduction at 24 h (SE 3.6), 67% at 48 h, 87% at 72 h; 3 mg/L: 45% at 24 h, 65% at 96 h; 30 mg/L: 99.4% by 6 h, 100% by 48 h. Toxins fell 60% at 5 mg/L in 24 h.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab). 5 mg/L is the practical threshold; the dip at 48 h suggests partial recovery at 5 mg/L.
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes.** Turmeric-extract powder, glass jars, a microscope with a Sedgwick-Rafter or haemocytometer slide. Swap *K. brevis* for a non-toxic dinoflagellate or *Dunaliella*/*Tetraselmis* (Form 3 low risk). Cheap, food-safe.
- **Read:** full text.

### 2. Curcumin in 1,400 L mesocosms with clams, urchins and crabs (Devillier et al., 2025/2026) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1002/lom3.10731 (*Limnology & Oceanography: Methods*)
- **What they did:** two Mote mesocosm runs (2022, 2024): 5 mg/L curcumin added to ~1.0 × 10^6 *K. brevis* cells/L in 1,400 L tanks. The 2024 run added nutrients, water clarity and hard clams (*Mercenaria*), sea urchins (*Lytechinus variegatus*) and blue crabs (*Callinectes sapidus*).
- **Environment:** turbidity rose significantly above background; no significant change in total dissolved N or P; no significant effect on animal mortality or behaviour after 72 h.
- **Results:** in both runs curcumin "significantly reduced algal cells and total toxins within 24 h". I could not get exact percentages (the publisher blocked access).
- **Effective?** Yes at mesocosm scale; the paper is framed as "lessons learned", so it also reports practical problems.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Partly. A 20-100 L tank version with brine shrimp or snails as the non-target check is feasible; blue crabs and clams are not needed.
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 3. How curcumin kills *K. brevis*: thioredoxin reductase (Taher, Liu, Hondal & Rein, 2025) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13198884/
- **What they did:** enzyme assays and LC-MS/MS on recombinant *K. brevis* thioredoxin reductase (KbTrxR).
- **Environment:** not tested (mechanism paper).
- **Results:** curcumin inhibits KbTrxR with IC50 3.0 µM, about 20× more potent than brevetoxin PbTx-2. It binds covalently to Cys53, turning the enzyme into a pro-oxidant.
- **Effective?** Partly. It explains the lab kill, but it also implies other organisms with the same enzyme could be affected.
- **Teen-reproducible?** No (enzymology). Useful as the "why it works" background.
- **Read:** abstract plus parts of the full text.

### 4. Curcumin breakdown products and non-target toxicity (Lima, Silva, da Silva, Machulek et al., 2022) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules27175699 (PMC9457702)
- **What they did:** sunlight-activated curcumin as a mosquito larvicide (4.6 mg/L, 21 days, simulated field). They identified 12 photodegradation intermediates by LC-MS and *predicted* their acute toxicity to *Daphnia*, fish and green algae.
- **Environment:** curcumin photodegrades in hours of light. The predicted toxicity of the products was low, and earlier curcumin-sugar formulations were reported non-toxic to *Daphnia magna* and zebrafish.
- **Results:** LT50 of 3 days for larvae at 4.6 mg/L; not toxic in the dark.
- **Effective?** Not a HAB study; it is the best available evidence on **persistence**: curcumin breaks down in light, and the predicted non-target toxicity is low.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes, as a side test: curcumin jars in sun versus dark, tracking the colour fade with a phone-camera colorimeter.
- **Read:** full text (the toxicity numbers are computer predictions, not animal tests).

### 5. Five Chinese-herb compounds, including curcumin, against *Chattonella marina* (Liu, He, Li, Liu et al., 2016) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-016-6755-5
- **What they did:** lab tests of evodiamine, curcumin, 4-methoxysalicylaldehyde, esculin and gramine on the fish-killing raphidophyte *C. marina*.
- **Environment:** not measured.
- **Results:** gramine was strongest (96 h LC50 0.51 mg/L). Hall et al. (2024) note this group dropped curcumin because it coloured the water.
- **Effective?** Partly. Curcumin worked but was not the most potent compound here.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Partly. Only curcumin is safe and easy to buy; gramine is a lab chemical.
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 6. Curcumin reduces shellfish-toxin uptake in mussels (Yuan, Duan, Liu, Li et al., 2021) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3390/toxins13080578
- **What they did:** green mussels (*Perna viridis*) fed toxic *Prorocentrum lima*, with and without curcumin.
- **Environment:** curcumin reduced digestive-gland damage, a positive non-target effect on shellfish.
- **Results:** less diarrhetic shellfish toxin accumulated, linked to suppressed AhR/HR96/CYP3A4 gene induction (the abstract gives no percentage).
- **Effective?** Partly. It protects shellfish rather than killing the bloom.
- **Teen-reproducible?** No (toxic alga, toxin assays).
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 7. CLEAR and Xtreme field test in Venice, FL canals (Mote Marine Lab, 2025) [press]
- **Link:** https://mote.org/news/mote-and-partners-conduct-first-of-its-kind-red-tide-mitigation-field-test-during-an-active-bloom/ ; https://www.wusf.org/environment/2025-02-20/new-tools-against-red-tide-show-success-rate-venice-canal-testing-mote
- **What they did:** 14 Feb 2025, first deployment during an active bloom of Mote's "natural biocide" CLEAR and Heartland's Xtreme in residential canals.
- **Environment:** Mote says it monitored water quality and marine life; no data published.
- **Results:** WUSF reports about 70% (search snippets say 70-80%) *K. brevis* reduction within about an hour; Mote's release gives no numbers.
- **Effective?** Unverified (press only). CLEAR's composition is not disclosed.
- **Teen-reproducible?** No (proprietary).
- **Read:** press release and news summary.

## B. Barley and other straws

### 8. Rutgers NJAES fact sheet FS1171, "Barley straw for algae control" [agency report / extension]
- **Link:** https://njaes.rutgers.edu/fs1171/
- **What they did:** extension guidance for ponds.
- **Environment:** "not known to cause harm to people or to any higher plants or animals"; do not exceed the maximum dose, or decaying straw depletes oxygen.
- **Results:** 225 lb (4-5 bales) per surface acre (range 110-450 lb/acre); small ponds 1 lb per 2,000 gal. Lag of "several weeks"; effective 4-6 months. Prevents new growth and "does not kill existing algae"; mixed results on filamentous and blue-green algae. A New Jersey trial found no effect on filamentous algae.
- **Effective?** Partly. Preventive only.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes. The small-pond dose converts directly to a jar/tank dose (1 lb/2,000 gal ≈ 60 mg/L).
- **Read:** full text.

### 9. ITRC HCB-1, barley and rice straw fact sheet with case studies [agency report]
- **Link:** https://hcb-1.itrcweb.org/barley-and-rice-straw/
- **What they did:** multi-state guidance; bales (<1 year old, fungicide-free) staked 1-1.5 months before the bloom at 7 bales/acre, plus a mid-summer top-up; 6-50 mg/L in lakes.
- **Environment:** "No impact on submerged plants or fish"; releases tannins (a drinking-water treatment issue). It is an **unregistered algaecide**, so individuals can use it but licensed applicators cannot. It is described as working in "low-salinity estuarine areas".
- **Results:** UK 6 ha lake: *Oscillatoria* went from 10,000 filaments/mL to undetectable in 3 weeks, and 29 other water bodies reported reductions. Aberdeen reservoir 1993-98 (6-28 g/m³, twice a year): substantial reduction. Rice straw and dried cypress leaves were also inhibitory (cypress beat barley in aquaria).
- **Effective?** Yes for prevention in many cases. It does not work once a bloom has started.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes (straw is cheap). Cypress leaves versus barley is an untested-at-scale comparison a student could run.
- **Read:** full text (web page).

### 10. Barley straw in the Chesterfield Canal, UK (Welch, Barrett, Gibson & Ridge, 1990) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02179780
- **What they did:** rotting barley straw in a disused canal; the first field study (Part I; Parts II-III covered lab and fungal decomposition).
- **Environment:** not reported in what I saw.
- **Results:** reduced filamentous algae; "shows promise as a long-term method".
- **Effective?** Yes (canal, filamentous algae).
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes (straw in tanks).
- **Read:** summary only (Semantic Scholar TLDR).

### 11. Barley straw in a Derbyshire reservoir (Everall & Lees, 1996) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/0043-1354(95)00192-1
- **What they did:** rotting straw at ~25-50 g/m³ in a disused UK supply reservoir.
- **Environment:** "No short term deleterious environmental impact" in the receiving streams; the effect was partly biological, because more grazers were present.
- **Results:** phytoplankton productivity and cyanobacterial dominance both significantly reduced; no nutrient removal was involved.
- **Effective?** Yes.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes, as a tank analogue.
- **Read:** abstract only (via ADS/ITRC summary).

### 12. Barley straw in an upland Scottish loch (Harriman, Adamson, Shelton & Moffett, 1997) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1080/09583159730983
- **What they did:** moored bales against gull-driven algal blooms.
- **Environment:** bales became habitat for benthic invertebrates; algal diversity unaffected.
- **Results:** inhibition began **6-10 months** after placement and lasted at least 18 months; numbers of the main species were reduced.
- **Effective?** Partly (slow, cold water).
- **Teen-reproducible?** Partly. The long lag is too slow for a science-fair timeline unless the straw is pre-aged.
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 13. Limited effects of barley straw in a Midwestern pond (Boylan & Morris, 2003) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1080/07438140309354091
- **What they did:** replicated limnocorrals inside a 1 ha Iowa pond, three straw doses, 14 weeks.
- **Environment:** zooplankton community structure **not affected**.
- **Results:** no consistent inhibition of planktonic or filamentous algae; likely cause is low oxygen inside the bales in stagnant ponds.
- **Effective?** No. Key negative US field result; oxygenation matters.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes: aerated versus unaerated straw jars directly tests their explanation.
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 14. Barley straw on 12 freshwater algae and six ponds (Ferrier, Butler, Terlizzi & Lacouture, 2005) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biortech.2005.01.021
- **What they did:** lab bioassays of straw liquor on 12 species, plus four treated and two control ponds for one season (Maryland).
- **Environment:** some algae were stimulated (*Selenastrum*, *Spirogyra*, *Oscillatoria*, *Navicula*).
- **Results:** inhibited *Synura*, *Dinobryon* and *Microcystis*; no effect on 5 species; ponds showed no difference from controls.
- **Effective?** Partly. It is selective in the lab and did nothing in these ponds.
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes.** A multi-species jar screen is the ideal design.
- **Read:** abstract only (via academia.edu and search summaries).

### 15. Maryland lakes: Williston Lake and the Poplar Island brackish lagoon (Sellner, EPA webinar 2014-2017) [agency report]
- **Link:** https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2017-05/documents/mitigation-maryland-cyanobacteria-blooms.pdf
- **What they did:** Williston Lake: drained 80%, then lined the lake and streams with 3-5 bales/acre, some inoculated with white-rot fungi (*Trametes versicolor*, *Ceriporiopsis*). Poplar Island: bales in a brackish dredge-spoil lagoon, 2013-2015.
- **Environment:** not reported.
- **Results:** Williston: "no blooms, only clear water through 2016", but the drawdown and freezing confound the result. Poplar Island: 2013 looked effective, then a 2014 bloom above 79 million cells/mL with microcystin >28 ppm. The conclusion there was that straw only helps combined with sediment capping. (ITRC cites 10× more inhibitory activity with fungal inoculation.)
- **Effective?** Partly. It failed alone in brackish water.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Partly. Fungus-inoculated versus plain straw extract is feasible (oyster-mushroom spawn is a safe white-rot fungus).
- **Read:** full text (slides).

### 16. Why barley works: microbes and hydrogen peroxide (Iredale, McDonald & Adams, 2012) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.watres.2012.08.040
- **What they did:** decomposition and bioassay experiments on straw.
- **Environment:** n/a.
- **Results:** microbial decomposition for ≥3 weeks is needed to release inhibitors; **finely chopping fresh straw releases them much faster**; activity was higher under UV-supplemented light and was reduced by catalase, which points to hydrogen peroxide. The outcome depended on test strain and temperature.
- **Effective?** Partly (mechanism).
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes.** Chopped versus whole straw, light versus dark, with and without catalase (a slice of raw potato is a safe catalase source).
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 17. Barley straw on dinoflagellates (Terlizzi, Ferrier, Armbrester & Anlauf, 2002) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1021164302634
- **What they did:** straw extract on dinoflagellates from three orders (Chesapeake-type estuarine species).
- **Environment:** some species stimulated.
- **Results:** inhibited *Gyrodinium galatheanum* (= *Karlodinium*), *Gymnodinium sanguineum*, *Heterocapsa triquetra* and *H. pygmaea*; **stimulated** *Prorocentrum minimum* and *P. micans*; no effect on others. "Not a universal management tool".
- **Effective?** Partly. This matters for Long Island Sound, where *Prorocentrum* is common.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes, with a non-toxic *Heterocapsa* or *Prorocentrum* culture.
- **Read:** abstract only (via search summary).

### 18. Barley straw on freshwater and brackish phytoplankton (Brownlee, Sellner & Sellner, 2003) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1023/B:JAPH.0000004353.15684.25
- **What they did:** straw exposure of freshwater and brackish taxa and mixed assemblages.
- **Environment:** shifts in species dominance in mixed communities.
- **Results:** species-specific responses (numbers not retrieved).
- **Effective?** Partly.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes (brackish water from a local estuary plus straw).
- **Read:** summary only (TLDR). Get the full text before citing.

### 19. Barley straw extract did not suppress *Prymnesium parvum* (Grover, Baker, Ureña-Boeck, Brooks et al., 2007) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.watres.2007.03.025
- **What they did:** 3-week batch cultures of the brackish fish-killer *P. parvum* at 10/20/30 °C with weekly straw-extract or ammonium doses; fish toxicity bioassays.
- **Environment:** n/a.
- **Results:** straw extract did not reduce growth rate, final density **or fish toxicity**; high ammonium did.
- **Effective?** No (for this brackish species). Hagström et al. (2010) similarly call straw ineffective against *P. parvum* and dinoflagellate blooms.
- **Teen-reproducible?** No (toxic alga, fish tests).
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 20. Barley straw in a hypertrophic French pond (Prygiel, Charriau, Descamps, Prygiel et al., 2014) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3846/16486897.2013.801847
- **What they did:** a full-year, high-frequency monitoring study of a straw-treated pond (chlorophyll 8-83 µg/L).
- **Environment:** the pond stayed well oxygenated.
- **Results:** straw "does not seem to hinder the growth of cyanobacteria", nor did it favour other algae.
- **Effective?** No.
- **Teen-reproducible?** n/a (it is a field monitoring study).
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 21. Camelina straw versus barley straw in aquaria (Świerk, Celewicz, Krzyżaniak, Antoszewski et al., 2025) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-82343-5 (open access)
- **What they did:** 15 aquaria (none / camelina / barley straw, n=5) with water from a eutrophic reservoir, 8 weeks.
- **Environment:** polyphenols **promoted filamentous green algae**.
- **Results:** camelina straw was the stronger inhibitor of chrysophytes, potentially toxic cyanobacteria and dinoflagellates, via gallic and caffeic acids and flavonoids.
- **Effective?** Partly.
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes.** This is almost exactly a science-fair design (aquaria, lake water, straw types).
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 22. A simple decomposed-barley extract (Ball, Williams, Vincent & Robinson, 2001) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/S0960-8524(00)00148-6
- **What they did:** 1% w/v barley or wheat straw in lake water for 28 days, then an aqueous extract of decomposed barley tested on *Microcystis* and *Scenedesmus*.
- **Environment:** n/a.
- **Results:** with barley, chlorophyll stayed below the starting 40 µg/L; with wheat it reached 1,160 µg/L. The extract (as dilute as 0.005%) gave ~10-fold lower chlorophyll.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab).
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes.** This is the cleanest protocol for experiment #2 (barley versus wheat as the control straw).
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 23. Rice straw extract on *Microcystis* (Park, Han, Ahn, Kim et al., 2006) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1472-765X.2006.01951.x
- **What they did:** rice straw extract at 0.01-10 mg/L, 8 days; seven phenolic compounds tested singly and in mixtures.
- **Environment:** n/a.
- **Results:** all doses decreased cell numbers; salicylic acid (0.1 mg/L) was the most active single compound (26%); the phenolics acted synergistically.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab).
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes (rice straw is easy to buy; the salicylic acid comparison is safe at mg/L).
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 24. Barley straw extract helps invasive water plants (2023) and its effects on duckweed (2014) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s11756-023-01550-z ; https://doi.org/10.1051/kmae/2014025
- **What they did:** 4-week lab exposure of *Elodea nuttallii* and *Cabomba caroliniana* to extract at 0.03-1.5 mL/L; a separate duckweed test.
- **Environment:** a **non-target effect**: the extract increased tillering and internodes in invasive plants. Duckweed biomass grew more slowly at the lower dose, and its roots grew longer.
- **Results:** if anything, straw extract benefits submerged invasive plants.
- **Effective?** n/a (these are non-target studies).
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes.** *Elodea* from a pet shop as a non-target control is easy and safe.
- **Read:** abstract only.

## C. Macrophyte (water-plant) allelopathy

### 25. Submerged plants inhibit phytoplankton (Körner & Nicklisch, 2002) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1529-8817.2002.t01-1-02001.x
- **What they did:** dialysis-bag co-cultures of *Myriophyllum spicatum*, *Ceratophyllum demersum* and *Potamogeton pectinatus* with cyanobacteria, a diatom and a green alga; PAM fluorometry.
- **Environment:** the plants are natural lake residents.
- **Results:** *Myriophyllum* and *Ceratophyllum* inhibited photosystem II and then growth; *Potamogeton* had none. *Microcystis* and Oscillatoriales were most sensitive; *Aphanizomenon*, the diatom and the green alga less so; inhibition scaled with plant biomass.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab), selectively.
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes.** Hornwort (*Ceratophyllum*) is sold for aquaria; a mesh or dialysis divider separates plant and algae.
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 26. Meta-analysis: how submerged plants suppress algae (Liu, Sun, Yang, Li et al., 2024) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvman.2024.120480
- **What they did:** a meta-analysis of lab and mesocosm studies.
- **Environment:** favours restoring plants rather than adding chemicals.
- **Results:** allelopathy beat shading and nutrient competition; cyanobacteria and diatoms were hit harder than green algae, and toxic cyanobacteria more than non-toxic; *Ceratophyllum*, *Vallisneria* and *Potamogeton* strongly inhibited *Microcystis*; co-culture beat extracts; mesocosm evidence is still thin.
- **Effective?** Yes (synthesis).
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes (it guides species choice).
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 27. A green alga can reverse macrophyte inhibition of *Microcystis* (Chang, Eigemann & Hilt, 2012) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hal.2012.06.002
- **What they did:** *Microcystis* exposed to macrophyte allelochemicals alone and with a competing green alga.
- **Environment:** a cautionary result on community interactions.
- **Results:** the green alga's presence reversed the inhibition, and the macrophytes could then *support* *Microcystis*.
- **Effective?** No, in mixed communities: single-species tests overstate the effect.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes: a mono-culture versus mixed-culture design.
- **Read:** summary only (TLDR).

## D. Seaweeds against marine HABs (most relevant to Long Island Sound)

### 28. Sea lettuce (*Ulva lactuca*) inhibits seven HAB species (Tang & Gobler, 2011) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hal.2011.03.003
- **What they did:** fresh *Ulva* thalli at environmentally realistic densities co-cultured with *Aureococcus* (brown tide), *Chattonella*, *Cochlodinium* (now *Margalefidinium*), *Karlodinium*, *K. brevis*, *Prorocentrum minimum* and *Pseudo-nitzschia*; the *Ulva* came from Old Fort Pond, Long Island (per Ben Gharbia et al. 2017's table).
- **Environment:** *Ulva* is native to Long Island Sound; it adds no foreign chemical.
- **Results:** dose-dependent lysis or strong inhibition of all seven species; the allelochemicals are heat-stable.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab).
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes.** Collect *Ulva* from a CT shoreline, use a non-toxic *Prorocentrum* or *Tetraselmis* culture, and count cells.
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 29. Sugar kelp, Irish moss and *Ulva* versus *Alexandrium* and mussel toxins (Sylvers & Gobler, 2021) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.hal.2021.102056
- **What they did:** lab co-cultures, bottle incubations of field bloom water and mesocosms stocked with aquacultured kelp (*Saccharina latissima*), *Chondrus crispus* and *Ulva*; a mussel saxitoxin test.
- **Environment:** framed as a non-invasive add-on to shellfish farms. It lowered saxitoxin in blue mussels (71.8 versus 93.5 µg/100 g, below the 80 µg closure limit).
- **Results:** cultures fell 17-74% in 2-3 days and 42-96% in about a week; field bloom populations fell by up to 95%. In the mesocosms, kelp cut *A. catenella* 73% in 48 h and *Ulva* cut it 54% in 96 h.
- **Effective?** Yes (mesocosm; Gobler lab, Stony Brook; the abstract does not name the field site). The strongest marine field-adjacent evidence in this family.
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes**, with a non-toxic dinoflagellate. CT has kelp farms that could supply sugar kelp.
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 30. Seagrass and *Ulva* against benthic and planktonic dinoflagellates (Ben Gharbia, Kéfi-Daly Yahia, Cecchi, Masseret et al., 2017) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0187963 (open access)
- **What they did:** co-cultures of fresh *Zostera noltei*, *Cymodocea nodosa* and *Ulva rigida* with *Ostreopsis*, *Prorocentrum lima*, *Coolia* and *Alexandrium pacificum* for 10 days.
- **Environment:** all natural Mediterranean residents.
- **Results:** *Ulva* was the strongest; the planktonic *Alexandrium* was the most vulnerable and benthic species more tolerant. Its table compiles earlier *Ulva* studies.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab, species-specific).
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes (eelgrass is protected in CT, so use *Ulva*).
- **Read:** full text.

### 31. *Ulva*, *Corallina* and *Sargassum* against *Heterosigma* and *Alexandrium* (Wang, Xiao, Zhang, Qu et al., 2007) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10811-006-9117-8 (PMC2668583)
- **What they did:** fresh tissue, dry powder, aqueous extracts and culture filtrate of three seaweeds tested on two microalgae.
- **Environment:** allelochemicals are "rapidly degradable"; a single filtrate dose did nothing, while repeated (semi-continuous) doses worked.
- **Results:** strong inhibition, lethal to *Heterosigma* at higher doses.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab). The persistence finding means the effect needs continuous release, which is short-lived once the seaweed is removed.
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes.** Dry seaweed powder versus fresh versus single versus repeated doses of soak water is a clean experiment.
- **Read:** abstract only.

## E. Plant extracts, polyphenols, tannins, essential oils

### 32. Crushed *Moringa* seeds kill *Microcystis* (Lürling & Beekman, 2010) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10811-009-9485-y (PMC2898099)
- **What they did:** filtrates of crushed *Moringa oleifera* seeds at 4-160 mg/L on *M. aeruginosa*, 14 days.
- **Environment:** the seeds release organic carbon and nutrients that could feed later regrowth (not seen within 14 days).
- **Results:** 4-8 mg/L had no effect; ≥20 mg/L gave a negative growth rate (-0.23/day); the 20 mg/L treatment **recovered**; ≥40 mg/L drove PSII efficiency to zero; dense blooms (~270 µg/L chlorophyll) were wiped out within 2 weeks at ≥80 mg/L.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab), at high doses.
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes.** Moringa seeds are sold as a supplement; measure by colour or chlorophyll.
- **Read:** full text.

### 33. Negative result: *Fructus mume*, *Salvia* and *Moringa* extracts, and lysine (Lürling & van Oosterhout, 2014) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3390/w6061807 (open access)
- **What they did:** a head-to-head test at 5-50 mg/L on *M. aeruginosa*.
- **Environment:** the *F. mume* effect came from low pH (organic acids), not a specific toxin.
- **Results:** only L-lysine (≥4.3 mg/L) cut biomass, and briefly: PSII **recovered after 6 days**. *F. mume* needed ≥240 mg/L; the others were "virtually non-effective".
- **Effective?** No. An important counterweight to many positive extract papers.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes, and a replication would be valuable.
- **Read:** full text.

### 34. Garlic and diallyl trisulfide on *Microcystis* (Wang, Wang, Ma & Xu, 2016) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-015-5809-4 ; related marine study: Zhou et al. 2008 on *Alexandrium tamarense*, https://doi.org/10.1007/s10811-007-9262-8
- **What they did:** fresh garlic and its compound diallyl trisulfide for 120 h.
- **Environment:** not measured; the authors call garlic "cheap, non-toxic".
- **Results:** EC50 0.75 g/L garlic and 2.84 mg/L diallyl trisulfide; up to 90% inhibition; Fv/Fm fell from 0.50 to 0.08 at 2 g/L.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab), but g/L doses would be huge in the field.
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes.** Kitchen garlic juice dose series.
- **Read:** abstract only (the 2008 marine paper: title only).

### 35. Pomegranate peel, luteolin and quercetin (Chen, Wang, Shi, Zhao et al., 2019; also quercetin, *Plants* 2023) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-019-05507-1 ; https://doi.org/10.3390/plants12091808
- **What they did:** peel powder and extract on *M. aeruginosa*; allelochemicals identified by HPLC-MS; the 2023 study tested quercetin at 10-40 mg/L.
- **Environment:** not measured.
- **Results:** luteolin 7-10 mg/L gave 98.7-99.1% inhibition at day 7. Quercetin 40 mg/L gave 90.8% lower cell density at 96 h, and chlorophyll fell 60-81% at 10-40 mg/L.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab).
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes.** Pomegranate peel is food waste; dry, grind and soak it.
- **Read:** abstract only (the quercetin paper is open access; I read its abstract).

### 36. Juglone (walnut) harms target algae and helps green algae, lab and lake enclosures (Park, Kim & Hwang, 2020) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3390/app10082873 (open access)
- **What they did:** juglone at 0.01-1 mg/L on 11 algae in the lab plus 7-day field enclosures in eutrophic Lake Ilgam, Seoul (August, 28-33 °C).
- **Environment:** a **non-target test**: green algae were *stimulated* and diatoms showed hormesis. In the enclosures cyanobacteria fell while green algae rose; species count dropped from 17 to 13 at 1 mg/L versus 16 in the control. A separate study (Jenv. Manage. 2019) found juglone raised microcystin per cell, though total microcystin did not rise.
- **Results:** *Microcystis* reduced 35-93%.
- **Effective?** Yes, selectively (field enclosures).
- **Teen-reproducible?** Partly. Juglone is a skin irritant; black-walnut hull tea is a gentler analogue.
- **Read:** full text.

### 37. Gallic and nonanoic acids in 3 m³ outdoor mesocosms with snails, amphipods and fish (Técher, Fontaine, Personne, Viot et al., 2016) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2015.12.164
- **What they did:** 13 outdoor mesocosms with local phytoplankton plus *Physa*, *Lymnaea*, *Gammarus* and rudd (*Scardinius*); gallic acid at 1, 2 and 4 mg/L, early in the season; 28 days.
- **Environment:** **no biomarker induction in any non-target animal**; other algae kept growing.
- **Results:** gallic acid limited cyanobacteria at 1 mg/L but did **not** eliminate them after repeated doses; nonanoic acid was weaker.
- **Effective?** Partly. The best-designed non-target study in this family.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Partly. Gallic acid is a cheap, low-hazard reagent (also found in tea and oak galls); pond snails as a non-target organism are easy.
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 38. Low gallic-acid doses *stimulate* *Microcystis* and toxin (Luo, Dao, Zhou, Wang et al., 2024) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2023.169765
- **What they did:** gallic acid from 10 mg/L down to 0.001 µg/L, simulating dilution after dosing.
- **Environment:** a regrowth and toxin **risk**.
- **Results:** at 0.1 µg/L the growth rate rose 1.09× and microcystin 1.85×; ecological risk quotient ~30× the control.
- **Effective?** No, at low dose: hormesis means that when the dose fades the bloom can come back stronger.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes (a wide dose series in jars).
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 39. Tannic acid suppresses *Microcystis* but can raise toxin release (Yin, Shi, Yin, Zhang et al., 2025) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhazmat.2025.137205
- **What they did:** long-term *M. aeruginosa* exposure to tannic acid with proteomics.
- **Environment:** a toxin-release risk.
- **Results:** high doses inhibited growth and microcystin-LR; at a particular intermediate dose, extracellular MC-LR peaked at **1.91× the control on day 15**. Related: eucalyptus tannins ≥80 mg/L gave >54.9% inhibition at day 4, and >75% with iron (Xie et al., 2023, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ecoenv.2022.114452).
- **Effective?** Partly.
- **Teen-reproducible?** Yes for growth (tea or oak-leaf tannins); toxin measurement needs ELISA strips (possible but costly).
- **Read:** abstract only (both).

### 40. Tannin coagulants are not acutely toxic to a copepod at working doses (J. Plankton Res., 2026; authors not retrieved) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1093/plankt/fbag004
- **What they did:** acute lethality of the tannin coagulants Tanfloc and *Mimosa* (Jurema) tannin, plus two phosphorus adsorbents, on the copepod *Notodiaptomus iheringi*.
- **Environment:** LC50 224 mg/L (Tanfloc) and 705 mg/L (Jurema); no significant lethality at 50 mg/L, the working dose.
- **Results:** a safety margin of about 4-14× for zooplankton.
- **Effective?** n/a (safety study). Supports tannins over aluminium coagulants, but these are floc-and-sink products (the counselor's retrieval objection still applies).
- **Teen-reproducible?** Partly. A copepod or *Daphnia* acute test is a good non-target add-on to any experiment here.
- **Read:** abstract only.

### 41. Seaweed essential oils against *Microcystis* (El Amrani Zerrifi, El Khalloufi, Mugani, El Mahdi et al., 2020) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.3390/toxins12080527 (PMC7472222)
- **What they did:** hydrodistilled essential oils of *Cystoseira tamariscifolia*, *Sargassum muticum* and *Ulva lactuca*; agar diffusion plus liquid culture for 6 days.
- **Environment:** not measured.
- **Results:** *Cystoseira* oil: inhibition zone 46 mm, MIC 7.81 µg/mL, >67% growth inhibition over 6 days; induced oxidative stress.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab).
- **Teen-reproducible?** Partly. The agar-diffusion ("zone of inhibition") method is very teen-friendly; making the oil needs a distillation set-up.
- **Read:** abstract only (full text available).

### 42. Slow-release allelochemical beads extend the effect (Huang, Xiao, Lin, Grossart et al., 2016) [peer-reviewed]
- **Link:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.watres.2016.02.058
- **What they did:** polymer beads loaded with the flavonoid 5,4'-dihydroxyflavone, versus direct dosing, on *Microcystis*.
- **Environment:** the beads stay in the water (retrieval issue), but release is slow and localised.
- **Results:** directly added compound worked at most 10 days; beads >30 days (theoretical release ~120 days); more effective at low (early-bloom) cell densities.
- **Effective?** Yes (lab), and it directly addresses **duration of effect**.
- **Teen-reproducible?** **Yes**, with a variant: calcium-alginate beads (sodium alginate plus calcium chloride, the "spherification" kit) loaded with curcumin or tea polyphenol, retrievable with a mesh bag.
- **Read:** abstract only.
