# Pair peroxide with a self-cleaning screen

The bio-inspired filters that get attention (fish gill-raker cross-step filters, manta-ray "ricochet" separation, baleen) have **never been shown to separate single 2-50 µm algal cells at device scale**. Every macro-scale model was tested on particles of about 100 µm to 1.6 mm. Even the original manta study found **minimal capture below about 200 µm**. The only versions that reach cell size are microfluidic chips that handle a few mL/min, about 6 orders of magnitude below what a harbor pen needs. Among the physical (non-chemical) options, only a **low-head, self-cleaning mesh screen (10-20 µm)** fits a small solar budget at useful flows. It removes about half of mixed plankton per pass at full scale, mostly the large cells and chains. Membranes, DAF, electrocoagulation and centrifuges remove more than 90%, but they need roughly 30-900 times the energy budget, and most of them quietly add a chemical. Ultrasound has negative field evidence. Algal turf scrubbers and floating wetlands rely on living organisms and cannot be switched on by a forecast. **Verdict:** one physical side experiment is worth adding: a pumped 20 µm mesh screen, run **dead-end vs cross-flow (the gill-raker principle used as an anti-clogging trick)**, tested on the same cell-size panel as the peroxide screen. The main reason is that its size selectivity is the mirror image of peroxide's. Peroxide kills the ~2 µm cells and spares the 20-50 µm dinoflagellates; a screen does the reverse. Frame it as a cheap complement measured in % removal per pass by size class and in Wh/m³, not as a "biomimetic HAB device". Everything else should be cited as a reasoned rejection.

Evidence labels used throughout: **[V]** means the research notes read the claim in the abstract or full text at the cited source. **[I]** means it is inference or arithmetic, either from the notes or added here. **[R]** means a widely quoted review number that was not re-read and should be checked before it is quoted.

## Bio-inspired filters fail below 100 µm because algae have no inertia

The fish-filter literature is real and elegant, but its particle range does not fit this problem. Sanderson et al. showed that suspension-feeding fish gill rakers act as **cross-flow filters**: high-velocity flow along the rakers carries particles toward the esophagus instead of trapping them on the filter **[V]** ([Sanderson et al. 2001, *Nature* 412:439, doi:10.1038/35086574](https://doi.org/10.1038/35086574)). The later **vortical cross-step** models were 3D-printed nylon covered with **140 µm mesh** and tested with **~250 µm *Artemia* cysts** at Re ≈ 4,200. Their strength is clog tolerance: covering 7% of the mesh cut retention by only 10.2%, against a 21% loss in conventional cross-flow models when 2.8% was covered **[V]** ([Sanderson et al. 2016, *Nature Communications* 7:11092, doi:10.1038/ncomms11092](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms11092)). The authors themselves note that particles smaller than the mesh pores "could remain suspended and be transported in the vortical crossflow" **[V]**. The closest HAB precedent, a rotating helical cross-step filter built for harmful algae, targeted ***Microcystis* colonies of 20-700 µm (median ~117 µm)**. It was tested with 106-125 µm microspheres, 6-12 mm slots and rotation of about 0.4 rev/s for anti-clogging, and reported its clogging result only qualitatively **[V]** ([Schroeder et al. 2019, *Bioinspiration & Biomimetics* 14:056008, doi:10.1088/1748-3190/ab2d13](https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-3190/ab2d13)). Witkop et al. showed that raker separation behaves like a **deterministic lateral displacement array**, with a geometric cut-off radius. They tested 0.94-1.64 mm particles through 1.35-1.8 mm gaps **[V]** ([Witkop et al. 2023, *Bioinspiration & Biomimetics* 18:056009, doi:10.1088/1748-3190/acea0e](https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-3190/acea0e)).

Manta-ray ricochet separation has the same limit. The models used 340 µm pores and 275 µm cysts at Re ≈ 300-750. Efficiency for particles smaller than the pores was **19 ± 5% (wing orientation) to 62 ± 5% (spoiler orientation)**. **Particles below ~200 µm showed minimal capture**, and no clogging was seen **[V]** ([Divi, Strother & Paig-Tran 2018, *Science Advances* 4:eaat9533, doi:10.1126/sciadv.aat9533](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aat9533)). A *PNAS* analysis of mobula-inspired leaky channels found that the cut-off size **falls only as flow speed rises**, with the vortex regime starting at ≥0.7 m/s. It tested 324 µm particles **[V]** ([Mao, Bischofberger & Hosoi 2024, *PNAS* 121:e2410018121, doi:10.1073/pnas.2410018121](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2410018121)). Mobulid CFD gives cut-off diameters of **250-600 µm** **[V]** ([Teeple et al. 2025, *Integr Comp Biol* 65:1576, doi:10.1093/icb/icaf142](https://doi.org/10.1093/icb/icaf142)). The concept does reach cell size, but only after it is shrunk to a chip. A manta "lobe" microfluidic filter reached **up to 99% at 20 mL/min** for ~10-30 µm particles **[V]** ([Clark & San-Miguel 2021, *Lab on a Chip* 21:3762, doi:10.1039/d1lc00449b](https://doi.org/10.1039/d1lc00449b)). A Dean-flow "gill-rake" chip reached **96.08% (10 µm) and 97.14% (15 µm) at 6 mL/min**, with a maximum throughput of 8 mL/min, and was also run on yeast **[V]** ([Hu et al. 2024, *Lab on a Chip*, doi:10.1039/d4lc00039k](https://doi.org/10.1039/d4lc00039k)).

The physics explains the gap. The Stokes number, St = ρd²U/(18μL), measures whether a particle can leave its streamline. With algal density ~1,050 kg/m³, U = 0.1-0.5 m/s and a 1 mm gap, the values are **[I]**:

| Particle | Stokes number | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| 5 µm cell | ~1.5e-4 to 7e-4 | follows the water like a tracer |
| 20 µm cell | ~0.002-0.012 | still essentially a tracer |
| 50 µm cell or chain | ~0.015-0.07 | weak inertia |
| 275 µm *Artemia* cyst (the actual test particle) | ~0.4-2.2 | ricochets |

Inertial separation needs St of roughly 0.1-1 or more **[I]**. For a 5 µm cell that means µm-scale gaps or water speeds of tens of m/s, which is microfluidic territory or would shear fragile dinoflagellates. One chip at 6-20 mL/min treats only about 9-29 L/day **[I]**. Animals that really do capture 1-50 µm particles, such as mussels (1-100 µm), sponges and ascidian mucus nets (down to ~1 µm), work at **Re ≈ 0.0002-50**. They catch particles by interception, mucus adhesion and cilia, not by inertia. A 2022 review notes that mucus-like adhesive filter media "are rarely used" in engineered solid-liquid filtration, and the only engineered spin-offs it cites are manta-inspired filters and the Schroeder helical algae filter **[V]** ([Hamann & Blanke 2022, *J R Soc Interface* 19:20210741, doi:10.1098/rsif.2021.0741](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2021.0741)). The engineered analogue of mucus capture is flocculation, which the project's earlier clay work already covers **[I]**. No peer-reviewed artificial mussel, sponge or choanoflagellate filter with measured microalgae removal turned up. A patent query in the Europe PMC index (manta, gill raker, cross-step or ricochet, plus filter) returned zero results **[V]** ([Europe PMC query](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=SRC:PAT%20AND%20(manta%20OR%20%22gill%20raker%22%20OR%20%22cross-step%22%20OR%20%22ricochet%22)%20AND%20filter&format=json)). Google Patents was not searched, so say "none found in the peer-reviewed literature or the Europe PMC patent index", not "none exist".

The one idea worth keeping is narrower: **the fish's cross-flow and vortex geometry keeps a fine mesh clear**. In the fish models a fine mesh does the actual retention **[V]**. A cross-flow or rotating arrangement could therefore protect a 10-20 µm mesh from blinding, and could also harvest floc or colonies of 100 µm or more (the Schroeder use case) **[I]**. That is the bio-inspired part a student can honestly test.

## Only a low-head screen fits a 1 kWh/day solar budget

Energy, not removal, decides this. Harvesting studies report kWh per kg at culture densities of 0.3-2 g/L, while a coastal bloom at 20-50 µg/L chlorophyll-a is only about **2-5 mg/L dry weight**. Energy scales with the volume pumped, so per-kg figures understate the cost for a HAB device 100-1,000-fold. The fair metric is **kWh per m³** **[I]**. A 10 × 10 × 3 m pen holds 300 m³. A ~200 W panel gives about 0.8-1 kWh/day, enough for one daily turnover only at about **0.003 kWh/m³**. Pumping costs about 0.0054 kWh/m³ per metre of head at 50% pump efficiency, so only a screen with less than ~0.5 m of head loss fits **[I]**.

| Method | Removal of 2-50 µm cells | Energy | Truly non-chemical? | Fits the device? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Drum microstrainer, 10-15 µm | **50.2-65.1%** plankton, 48.6-65.9% cyanobacteria (4-yr averages) **[V]** | low-head pumping, ~0.005 kWh/m³ per m of head **[I]** | yes | **yes**, partial removal |
| Cross-flow / submerged MF-UF membrane | >98% **[V]** | 0.77-2.06 kWh/m³ **[V]** | backflush plus chemical cleaning needed **[V]** | no (energy, membrane area) |
| Hydrocyclone (mini, 3D-printed) | 7.13x concentration of ~10 µm *Tetraselmis* **[V]** | ~0.3 kWh/m³ **[R]** | yes | no (cut size too large) |
| DAF | 87% to >95% **[V]** | ~1.5 kWh/m³ **[R]** | no (coagulant or polymer) **[V]** | no |
| Electrocoagulation, Al anode | 95-99.5% **[V]** | 0.3-2.65 kWh/kg **[V]** | no (doses Al in situ) | no |
| Inert-anode electroflotation in seawater | <10% to 96% (depends on hydrophobicity) **[V]** | 3.1 kWh/m³ **[V]** | no (makes chlorine) **[V]** | no |
| Ultrasound | no field effect **[V]** | n/a | yes | no |

Sources for the table: microstrainers ([Czyżewska & Piontek 2019, *Toxins* 11:285, doi:10.3390/toxins11050285](https://doi.org/10.3390/toxins11050285)); membranes ([Danquah et al. 2009, *J Chem Technol Biotechnol* 84:1078, doi:10.1002/jctb.2137](https://doi.org/10.1002/jctb.2137); [Bilad et al. 2012, *Bioresour Technol* 111:343, doi:10.1016/j.biortech.2012.02.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biortech.2012.02.009); [Mora-Sánchez et al. 2023, preprint, doi:10.20944/preprints202312.0254.v1](https://doi.org/10.20944/preprints202312.0254.v1)); hydrocyclone ([Shakeel Syed et al. 2017, *Lab on a Chip* 17:2459, doi:10.1039/c7lc00294g](https://doi.org/10.1039/c7lc00294g)); DAF ([Yap et al. 2014, *Water Research*, doi:10.1016/j.watres.2014.05.032](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.watres.2014.05.032); [Henderson, Parsons & Jefferson 2008, *Environ Sci Technol*, doi:10.1021/es702649h](https://doi.org/10.1021/es702649h)); electrocoagulation ([Vandamme et al. 2011, *Biotechnol Bioeng* 108:2320, doi:10.1002/bit.23199](https://doi.org/10.1002/bit.23199); [Pandey et al. 2020, *Environ Sci Pollut Res*, doi:10.1007/s11356-019-06897-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11356-019-06897-y); [Lin & Sidik 2024, *Water Research*, doi:10.1016/j.watres.2024.121868](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.watres.2024.121868)); electroflotation ([Qi et al. 2022, *Sci Total Environ*, doi:10.1016/j.scitotenv.2022.155866](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2022.155866); [Dennis, Karns & Posewitz 2026, *RSC Advances* 16:2449, doi:10.1039/d5ra07757e](https://doi.org/10.1039/d5ra07757e)); ultrasound ([Tischer et al. 2025, *J Environ Manage*, doi:10.1016/j.jenvman.2025.125057](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvman.2025.125057)). The [R] numbers come from [Milledge & Heaven 2013, *Rev Environ Sci Biotechnol* 12:165, doi:10.1007/s11157-012-9301-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11157-012-9301-z) and should be checked against its tables.

Three findings sharpen the verdict. First, the "electro" and flotation methods are not physical in the sense the project needs. Sacrificial-anode electrocoagulation is coagulant dosing produced electrically: process water carries <2 mg/L Al **[V]** (Vandamme 2011). Inert anodes in seawater make chlorine, which Dennis et al. call "a major issue" **[V]**. A Korean red-tide patent explicitly keeps free chlorine at ≤1 ppm **[V]** ([Cha et al. 2007, KR patent](https://europepmc.org/search?query=RED-TIDE%20REMOVAL%20DEVICE%20USING%20A%20SEA-WATER%20ELECTROLYSIS)). The reported *Microcystis* "electrolysis" kills are driven by oxidants, including electro-generated **H₂O₂ up to 58 mg/L** **[V]** ([Zhang et al. 2023, *Environ Pollut*, doi:10.1016/j.envpol.2023.121316](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.envpol.2023.121316)). So the electro route is a costlier, less controllable path to the oxidant the project already doses with a pump. Second, the one agency-scale pump-through system, the US Army Corps HABITATS (booms, weir skimmer, DAF barge, flocculant), was modelled at **US$3.88-7.32 M for a 90-day deployment**, with **25-87% removal** and a net societal benefit of **−$2.1 M to +$0.83 M** **[V]** ([Boubacar et al. 2024, USGS SIR 2024-5091, doi:10.3133/sir20245091](https://doi.org/10.3133/sir20245091)). Open-water removal is a losing game even for the government. A student device must target small, enclosed volumes and report per-pass numbers. Third, ultrasound is out. Independent field tests found "no music in controlling cyanobacteria in situ" **[V]** ([Lürling & Tolman 2014, *Water Research*, doi:10.1016/j.watres.2014.08.043](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.watres.2014.08.043)), and a five-reservoir comparison with control reservoirs found no significant differences **[V]** (Tischer 2025).

The screen's weakness is cell size and clogging. A 10 µm mesh lets most single 2-10 µm cells through and holds chains and 20-50 µm dinoflagellates. The Zawada plant's 50-65% removal **rose with raw-water plankton density** **[V]** (Czyżewska & Piontek 2019), which favours bloom conditions. On clogging, a 50 µg/L chl-a bloom carries about 5 g dry biomass per m³. A static 1-5 µm bag would blind within hours to a day, not two weeks **[I]**. Fine filtration on an unattended float therefore needs a self-cleaning element: a spray-washed drum, a reverse-flow pulse, or a cross-flow sweep with a sludge bag for the wash-off **[I]**. That is exactly where the gill-raker idea earns a role.

## Living and passive nature-based options fail the switchability rule

Algal turf scrubbers and floating treatment wetlands have real field records, but they are continuous nutrient-removal systems built on living biomass. An attached-algae flow-way in Texas's Upper Laguna Madre (a lagoon with recurrent brown tide) ran for **two years**. It recovered **300-500 mg N and 15-30 mg P per m² per day** **[V]** ([Kim et al. 2021, *Water Research*, doi:10.1016/j.watres.2021.116816](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.watres.2021.116816)). At that rate, removing 1 kg N/day needs about 2,000 m² **[I]**. Floating wetlands of **40-280 m²** in the Baltimore, Boston and Chicago harbors ran for over three years. They removed about **2 g P per m² per year** and produced only "localized changes" in bloom-forming cyanobacteria **[V]** ([Rome et al. 2023, *Sci Total Environ* 877:162669, doi:10.1016/j.scitotenv.2023.162669](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2023.162669)). Neither can be switched on by a forecast, both need live organisms, and both act over seasons. That puts them in the same bucket as the shellfish bags the project already dropped on 2026-10-01. At most, they belong in the discussion as season-long partners in pens the forecast repeatedly flags as high risk **[I]**.

Bio-inspired anti-fouling does matter, but for the device's sensors, not for removing algae. Biofouling can corrupt optical measurements "sometimes in less than a week" **[V]** ([Delauney, Compère & Lehaitre 2010, *Ocean Science* 6:503, doi:10.5194/os-6-503-2010](https://doi.org/10.5194/os-6-503-2010)). Commercial fluorometers rely on **wipers plus copper** (YSI EXO, Sea-Bird ECO, Turner C-series), with UV-C LEDs as the upgrade. One UV-C system protected a sensor for **nine months**. Applying biomimetic coatings to sensors is reported as "non-existent" in practice **[V]** ([Delgado, Briciu-Burghina & Regan 2021, *Sensors* 21:389, doi:10.3390/s21020389](https://doi.org/10.3390/s21020389)). Slippery liquid-infused surfaces reached over 90% fouling suppression after 180 days, but on field panels, not on sensor windows **[V]** ([Guo et al. 2026, *Langmuir*, doi:10.1021/acs.langmuir.6c01350](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.langmuir.6c01350)). For a two-week service interval, copper tape plus a wiper is enough on current evidence. A slippery coating would be lost to wiper abrasion, so test it at most as a half-coated window, as a curiosity **[I]**. Magnetic separation was not researched in these notes beyond the project's earlier clay-and-magnet design. It is a dosing-plus-retrieval method, so it does not count as a non-chemical option here.

## A $60 dead-end vs cross-flow mesh test fills peroxide's blind spot

The strongest argument for a screen is not biomimicry but **complementarity**. The project's own peroxide plan expects 0.8 mg/L pulses to kill only ~2 µm cells. Larger cells such as *P. micans* lost only 11% even at 6.4 mg/L (project notes, `notes/mitigation/EXECUTION_PLAN.md`). A 20 µm screen should behave the opposite way: it should strip chains and 20-50 µm dinoflagellates and let picoplankton through **[I]**. Together they would cover the whole 2-50 µm range, and both are relay-switchable with no live organisms. That makes a clean, testable secondary hypothesis that fits the existing cell-size panel without new cultures.

The bench test needs little equipment. Build one housing from PVC or a 3D print around **20 µm nylon plankton mesh** (10 µm as a second size if the first works), fed by the same kind of 12 V pump the loop already uses. It runs in two configurations. **Dead-end:** the mesh sits across the flow. **Cross-flow:** the mesh forms the wall of a tube, water sweeps tangentially along it, permeate passes through, and a small bleed of concentrate goes to a sludge bag. Run each configuration on single-species 10 L batches of the panel species (*Nannochloropsis*, *Micromonas*, *Skeletonema*, *Akashiwo* or *Prorocentrum*), n = 3, at a bloom-level density. Add a **pump-only, no-mesh control** to separate removal from shear damage, which matters because pumps and valves are known to damage fragile marine microalgae **[V]** ([Vandanjon et al. 1999, *Biotechnol Bioeng* 63:1, doi:10.1002/(SICI)1097-0290(19990405)63:1<1::AID-BIT1>3.0.CO;2-K](https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1097-0290(19990405)63:1%3C1::AID-BIT1%3E3.0.CO;2-K)). Measure four things. The first is **single-pass removal**: fluorometer readings in and out, with hemocytometer counts. The second is **removal by size class**, using the 5 µm syringe-filter split already in the peroxide protocol. The third is **clogging**: timed-bucket flow rate every 10 minutes over a 2-hour run, giving the time to a 50% flow drop. The fourth is **energy**: pump volts × amps ÷ flow, reported in Wh/m³ and checked against the 0.003 kWh/m³ budget. Pre-register two predictions **[I]**. Removal of 20-50 µm cells should exceed 70% per pass while *Nannochloropsis* and *Micromonas* removal stays below 20%. Cross-flow should take at least twice as long as dead-end to reach a 50% flow drop. Add a **cell-lysis check** on the retained material, either a lysis proxy or a toxin kit if budget allows: filters that trap toxic cells can release toxins, and sand filtration studies show cell-bound metabolites leaking from caught cells **[V]** ([Rougé et al. 2026, *Environ Sci Technol*, doi:10.1021/acs.est.5c16532](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.5c16532)). Mesh, fittings and a spare pump should cost well under $100 **[I, unverified prices]**. The whole test fits in about two days inside the Oct 26-Nov 14 screen window.

Expect partial, size-dependent removal: probably 40-70% bulk per pass in a mixed culture, on the Zawada pattern, and little effect on the 2 µm species **[I]**. The risks are that small cells pass and the claim shrinks to "large-cell taxa only", that dead-end mesh blinds within minutes at culture density, which makes the cross-flow comparison more interesting rather than less, and that pump shear could lyse dinoflagellates. If cross-flow does not outlast dead-end, the bio-inspired framing goes, and the result still informs the device as a plain screen. Do not claim bloom prevention or open-water control, which matches the project's standing rule. Report per-pass removal and Wh/m³ in a tank.

## Conclusion

For microalgae, "biomimetic" is mostly a size mismatch. The famous non-clogging mechanisms belong to particles ten times larger than a HAB cell, and the organisms that do catch 2-50 µm cells use sticky, slow, low-Re capture that engineers reproduce as flocculation or fine mesh. The most useful fact for the project is that filtration's inherent bias toward large cells exactly fills the gap the project's own peroxide data expose. A cheap screen therefore earns its place as a measured complement to peroxide that covers the large cells, and the gill-raker principle earns a single, falsifiable role as an anti-clogging geometry. Neither claim needs the word "bio-inspired" to hold.

The open uncertainties are concrete. No study gives a grade-efficiency curve for drum or disc microscreens on marine dinoflagellates by cell size. No energy-per-m³ figure for small aquaculture drum filters was retrieved. No macro-scale cross-step or ricochet geometry has been swept below 100 µm, so the cut-off curve in this report is inferred from physics, not measured. Before the write-up, run a Google Patents search and check the ISEF abstracts database for prior student "biomimetic algae filter" projects, so that any novelty claim is worded safely.
